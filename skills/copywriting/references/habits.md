# Habits that cost the reader

Language models and hurried writers share the habits below. Each is described by what the sentence does to the reader, so you can recognise it in any language. The examples are illustrations in English and Danish. Look for the move, whatever words carry it.

The repair is the same every time: return to what the reader needs to know at that place, and say it plainly, from the facts. Swapping one stock phrase for another keeps the habit. Each habit also has a proper use, so judge every instance by whether it serves this reader here.

## 1. Performing

The sentence is built to land. It closes a paragraph by restating it as a maxim, stands alone as a fragment for effect, or sets up a reveal with a question or a label ("The hard part?", "Here is what surprised us.", "The result is…").

Test it by deleting it. If the reader loses no information, it stays deleted. If they lose some, state that information in an ordinary sentence.

Proper use: a headline, an advertising line, one deliberate emphasis where the content calls for it.

## 2. Starting from what something is not

The text denies an idea the reader never had, then corrects it: "This is not just a tool. It is a platform." The reader has to build the wrong idea, discard it and build the right one.

State what the thing is. If a limit matters, give it afterwards as a plain fact.

Proper use: the reader really holds the mistaken view, or faces a real choice between the two.

## 3. Leaked context

The text leans on something only the writer has seen. Step 2 of the skill lists the forms. Two more belong here: sentences addressed to the person who asked for the text (an opening acknowledgement, a closing offer of more help), and mentions of drafts, changes or instructions.

Put on the page what the reader needs, and move the rest to your note to the user.

## 4. Telling the reader how to react

An opener rates what follows ("Interestingly,", "Importantly,"). An adverb vouches for sincerity or strength ("genuinely", "incredibly"). A sentence claims significance, or a trailing clause interprets a fact for the reader ("…, underlining its commitment to quality").

Give the fact that would cause the reaction, and let the reader have it.

Proper use: a judgement with its criterion stated ("the fastest of the four we tested").

## 5. Saying it more than once

A short text previews its content, delivers it and sums it up. A reply restates the question before answering. Two neighbouring sentences carry one idea in different words. A final paragraph repeats the body.

Say it once, in the place where the reader needs it.

Proper use: the summary at the top of a long document that people navigate.

## 6. Filling a pattern

Items come in threes when the material holds two or four. Every paragraph has the same length and shape. Every bullet opens with a bold label. A text short enough to read straight through gets headings.

Let the content set the count and the shape. This habit tends to grow when the others are removed, so check for it last.

## 7. Covering everything

The text lists every consideration without ranking them, supplies background this reader has, attaches a caveat to each claim, or lays out all sides when the reader asked what to do.

Choose. Say what matters most and what follows from it. Keep the caveat that would change what the reader does.

Proper use: the reader asked for an inventory.

## 8. Abstract where a specific exists

Abstract nouns do the work of people and actions ("the implementation of the solution"). Authorities go unnamed ("experts say"). An example would fit any subject. A formal word stands where a common one would do.

Name who did what, name the source, and use the example from this case. When you lack the specific, mark the gap as `[NEED: …]` for the user.

## 9. Miscalibrated certainty

Qualifiers pile up around a claim, or the claim is stated more firmly and more widely than its source allows ("some users" becomes "users").

State once how sure the claim is, with the reason.

## 10. Compressing past clarity

Articles and connecting words are dropped, arrows and symbols replace sentences, abbreviations go undefined, nouns stack up.

Write full sentences. The reader's time is saved by leaving things out, and lost when the words that remain are hard to parse.

## Examples

Before: "Remote work isn't about where you sit. It's about trust. And the results? They speak for themselves."
After: "Since the team went remote in March, it has shipped every release on schedule."

Before: "Det handler ikke om at arbejde hurtigere. Det handler om at arbejde klogere. Resultatet? Færre fejl."
After: "Alle fejlmeldinger samles nu ét sted, så teamet retter de fleste fejl samme dag."

Before: "As discussed, the updated flow now uses Option B, which is much simpler."
After: "From 1 May, customers confirm their order with one click on the summary page."

Before: "Importantly, the migration was completed successfully, highlighting the team's dedication."
After: "The migration finished on Sunday with no downtime."

Each rewrite needed a fact the first version lacked. When you lack it, ask the user.

## After revising

Read the passage once more and check three things: no new habit has taken the place of the old one, every fact, number and condition of the original is still there, and nothing has been added that the material does not support.
